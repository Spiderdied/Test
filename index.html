Grammar Reference
ARTICLES

What is an Article?

An article is an adjective. Like adjectives, articles modify nouns. English has two articles: a/an and the. A/an is used to modify non-specific or non-particular nouns; the is used to modify specific or particular nouns. We call a/an the indefinite article and the the definite article.

INDEFINITE ARTICLE

A / An

The indefinite article is an article that does not refer to a specific person or thing — it is used when it is not clearly understood who or what is meant. The form a is used before a word beginning with a consonant, or a vowel sounded like a consonant: a man, a university, a useful thing. The form an is used before words beginning with a vowel or a mute h: an apple, an hour, an honest man. The indefinite article is the same for all genders.

  • Before a singular countable noun mentioned for the first time representing no particular person or thing
  • Before a singular countable noun used as an example of a class
  • Before names of jobs or professions
  • In certain expressions of quantity: a lot of, a dozen, a hundred
  • In exclamations before singular countable nouns: What a nice day!
DEFINITE ARTICLE

The

The definite article refers to a specific person, place or thing. It is the same for singular and plural and for all genders: the man, the men; the woman, the women.

  • When the object is unique or considered to be unique: the sun, the moon
  • Before a noun that has become definite as a result of being mentioned a second time
  • Before a noun made definite by the addition of a phrase or clause
  • Before superlatives and ordinal numbers
  • Before nationality words: the Egyptians, the Chinese
  • Before names of musical instruments, rivers, seas, oceans, and chains of mountains
ZERO ARTICLE

No Article

We use no article in front of nouns like school and hospital when referring to their normal purpose: Aly has gone to school (to learn). Other nouns like this are: bed, church, class, college, prison, sea, town, university, work. However, we use the or a/an with these nouns when not referring to their purpose: Aly has gone to the school for a meeting.

SIMPLE PRESENT

The Simple Present Tense

A present verb form that has no auxiliary verb in the affirmative. It is mainly used to express permanent situations or habitual actions — things that happen regularly, repeatedly, or all the time. It is often used with adverbs such as always, usually, sometimes, often, never.

  • Permanent situations and habitual actions: It never rains here in August.
  • Timetabled future events: My plane leaves at 4 p.m.
  • With non-progressive verbs: I believe you.
  • In conditional sentences (type 1): If it rains, I will stay at home.
  • In time clauses: I'll stay here until you get back.
PRESENT CONTINUOUS

The Present Continuous Tense

A verb form made with am/is/are + …ing. It is formed with the present tense of the auxiliary verb to be and the present participle (infinitive + ing).

  • An action happening now (at the moment of speaking): What are you doing?
  • An action happening around this time but not necessarily at this moment: My nephew is working in Saudi Arabia at the moment.
  • A definite arrangement in the near future: What are you doing tomorrow evening?
  • With always for a frequently repeated action that annoys the speaker: His son is always going away for weekends.
NON-PROGRESSIVE VERBS

Common Non-progressive (Stative) Verbs

These verbs cannot be used in the continuous form. The simple present must be used instead.

  • Mental states: believe, know, understand, think, imagine, doubt, suppose, recognize, remember, forget
  • Emotions: like, love, hate, dislike, wish, want, prefer, please
  • Senses: see, hear, smell, taste, feel, sound, appear, look
  • Possession: have, own, possess, belong, contain, consist, include
  • Other: be, seem, need, matter, mean, measure, weigh, owe, promise
GOING TO vs WILL

Will and Going to

We use will when we decide to do something at the time of speaking — the decision is made at that moment. We use going to when we have already decided to do something before the moment of speaking. For predictions, we use going to when there is something in the present situation that makes the future outcome clear (e.g., Look at those black clouds! It's going to rain.). Otherwise it is safer to use will.
